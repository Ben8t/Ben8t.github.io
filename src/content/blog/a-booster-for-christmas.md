# A Booster for Christmas

Tom was so eager to open the presents. They were all waiting under the Christmas tree, a moment we had missed last year.
The whole family was there: Uncle Ben, his wife, my cousins and, most importantly, my grandmother.

She was a bit off that day, but at least she was there. I was very grateful for that.

We had just finished dinner and, as usual, she started scrolling on her phone. I never really understood why she still did that. I guess it was a habit she had picked up early in life.
All the elderly did it, so I felt there was no point fighting it.

We had tried to get her to use boosters, but she never saw why she needed them, let alone how they worked.
Sure, she knew how to plug one into her phone to generate TV shows, and she sometimes talked with it, but she never got the point.

Since her last operation, she had never fully gotten back in shape. Her brain was sometimes very slow, and we couldn't talk with her for long. Scrolling was probably her way of calming down when her surroundings got too noisy and tiring.

Pressed by Tom and my cousins, my dad finally let everyone leave the table so we could open the presents.

I already knew what one of mine was: a new set of boosters for my shoes. I'd gotten one for my jackets last year, and I was excited to finally complete my whole wardrobe.

My grandmother used to give me tips and original ideas on fashion design and on how to mix my clothes properly. A lot of my friends in high school were jealous of that.
Almost everybody had boosters for their clothes now, but you needed some real work (and advice from a wise grandmother) to stand out!

Fashion design was my true passion, but I still didn't know what to do after high school. It had been easier for my parents; college was still a thing in their time. With the intelligence revolution, most education systems had dropped the later years of study. The job market had split four ways: finding ways for work to be verified; new sources of energy and their optimization; work that couldn't be verified (mostly creative and curation activities); and the physical jobs robots hadn't taken over yet.

I was good enough at school to escape the last one, but design internships were hard to find. So, on Uncle Ben's advice, I chose business economics as my major.

I really liked how it mixed human psychology with economics. Most of the cases we studied were about executives and managers struggling to make their business “verifiable”. That was the new word in town, born from the intelligence revolution of the last few years. Our teacher put it simply: the digital revolution at the start of the century had let companies automate what they could specify; now they could automate what they could verify. If a booster could try a task, get an automatic score, start over and try again thousands of times a night, it would end up better at it than any of us. If it couldn’t, it could only copy people and hope for the best.

My favorite example was a clothing retailer. The fit was easy to verify: a garment fit a body scan or it didn’t, and boosters could practice on millions of simulated bodies. Tailors and fitting rooms disappeared almost overnight. Style was another story. Nobody could tell their machine whether an outfit was good, because that was taste, and taste changed from one person, and one season, to the next. And so the stylists stayed in business.

I loved that case as it reminded me of my grandmother: nobody had ever verified her advice; it just worked. The bottleneck wasn’t the boosters, it was people’s understanding of their own business. Before you can check whether something is good, you have to know what good is. I already had my thesis title: “You can’t verify what you don’t understand.”

"Oh! Lego Boosters!! I've waited so long for them!!"
Tom had just opened the Lego set Santa had brought him.

We had heard that our cousins got a similar set last year, and that Aunt Aubrey and Uncle Ben had struggled with the mess that came with it.

"Let's do a battle!"
My cousins had brought their own set and were eager to set up the scene for "their campaign."

It was a trendy toy for boys that age. My dad helped Tom build the first piece and plug in the booster, so the pieces could come to life and build the rest of the set themselves.
Calibration took two minutes. Soon it was moving around Tom, asking him what to do.

"Fight George's one!"
As expected, the living room soon looked like a battlefield, with Lego pieces getting smashed everywhere...

It was nice to have everyone home. Last year, our family couldn't get together because of all the terrorist attacks and the flight cancellations that followed.
Several planes had been hijacked because the airlines hadn't upgraded their fleets. Boosters were still new enough that industries with that much logistical inertia couldn't move fast enough.
We had learnt at school that any USB drive could easily host a booster, compromising the entire host.
In history lessons, we had seen pictures of boosters from the early '20s that were as big as a shoebox.
Now they were the size of a spoon, so they were easy to hide.
In that same class, we even learnt they could get smaller still in the future. They called that Moore's Law, I think.

"Tom!! Stopppp!! It can't get this crazy the second you open your toy!"
"You know your grandmother doesn't like to be overstimulated!"

Things were getting out of hand in the living room. My grandmother had just been hit by a Lego piece, and my mom was bringing out dessert.

She was wearing her FineTuner gloves, as she did for all sorts of daily chores.
I had overheard her and my dad saying they had been an expensive buy. The robot that came with them was still in the garage, waiting for training data.
Our family was late to this stuff. My friends had had robots doing chores at home for months. The idea was simple, though: wear the FineTuner gloves for your daily chores so the robot could learn every detail of your home and furniture, then train a dedicated model so it could do the chores itself.

My grandmother seemed tired. She really didn't like it when too many things happened at once. She slowly moved to the sofa, away from my cousins and all the noise. Once settled, she started scrolling on her phone again.

Uncle Ben turned on the TV and plugged in its booster.
We were huge fans of family activities, and playing games together was probably the best moment of Christmas.
That night's game was called Multipoly; Uncle Ben had made it during their flight, based on one of his childhood board games.

Our personal boosters explained the basics to each of us, and we started playing.
I had played lots of games with my grandmother, and the anecdotes she told me about them had helped me build a strong custom strategy model for my booster.

After fifteen minutes, I was already far ahead. My booster kept giving me tips on how to make money in the game. Too bad for Uncle Ben: he was yelling instructions at his booster for better strategies, and all he got was slop.

"Grandmother!"

My grandmother had just fallen off the sofa. Between my cousins' army of almost twenty Lego fighters and the noisy game, with everyone asking their booster to change strategy, she had probably taken in too many stimuli.

"It's probably like the last time," said my dad, not too worried.

"I hope so..." said my mom, more concerned.

It was true that since her implant, she had to be careful about her environment, something she never really got used to after a life on her phone.

My dad went over to help her.

"Give me a booster. I'll clear her context."
